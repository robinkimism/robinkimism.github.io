I've seen the world in numbers since kindergarten — box scores, batting averages, whatever had a stat line. That habit followed me from Korea to Frisco, Texas in 2024, and eventually turned into a question I couldn't let go: my high school team had some of the best pitching in the district last year — second and third in team ERA — and still missed the playoffs. Somewhere between what the numbers said and what actually happened on the field, something got lost.

That question became my ISM research: how much of MLB-level analytics actually holds up at the high school level, and what gets missed when it doesn't. I study math and statistics, play varsity baseball, and split my research time between physics-based performance modeling and predictive umpire-tendency analysis. I also intern with Diegobaseball and write bilingual (English/Korean) baseball analytics content on the side.

Numbers got me here. I'm trying to figure out where they stop being enough.

Topic Proposal

Research Question: To what extent do MLB-level sabermetric strategies apply to high school baseball?

Liberty High School's baseball team posted the second- and third-best team ERA in the district last season — yet missed the playoffs entirely. That gap between measurable performance and game outcome is the starting point for this research: MLB front offices operate with specialist pitching staffs, 162-game sample sizes, and precise tracking infrastructure. High school programs have none of that — smaller rosters, roughly 20-game seasons, players splitting time across multiple positions, and far less access to advanced tracking technology.

This project investigates which MLB-derived metrics and tactics transfer meaningfully to the high school level, which require significant adaptation, and which don't hold up once sample size, athlete development stage, and resource gaps are accounted for. The research draws on peer-reviewed sports medicine and sabermetrics literature, physics-based performance modeling (exit velocity, launch angle, trajectory), and original analysis of high school umpire strike-call tendencies.

Program

About ISM

Independent Study & Mentorship (ISM) is a self-directed research course. Rather than following a set curriculum, each student identifies a real question in a field they care about, builds the background knowledge to investigate it seriously, and eventually pairs with a professional mentor already working in that space. The year moves from topic selection and literature review toward original research and a final product — presented publicly at the end of the program.

For this project, that means treating baseball analytics as a genuine open question rather than an assumed conclusion: testing, source by source, where MLB-level sabermetrics actually holds up at the high school level — and where it doesn't.
